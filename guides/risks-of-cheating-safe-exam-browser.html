Cheating in a Safe Exam Browser exam: the risks to you

Published 2026-11-26 · Unofficial guide for students

Students searching for ways around Safe Exam Browser (SEB) often assume the worst that can happen is a bad mark. The real risks are much larger, and some of them have nothing to do with your university. This page is about those risks. It does not explain how to get round SEB, and nothing here is a challenge to try.

Your university’s regulations come first. Penalties and procedures differ between institutions. Read your own academic misconduct policy and your exam instructions.

What academic misconduct can cost you

Universities treat cheating in an exam as serious academic misconduct. Depending on the case and the institution, outcomes can include:

  • a mark of zero for the exam or the whole module, even if you had passed it honestly;
  • having to retake a module, or a capped mark on a resit;
  • a formal warning or a note on your student record;
  • suspension, or being removed from your programme;
  • for a degree that leads to a profession (such as medicine, teaching, law, engineering or nursing), a misconduct finding that you may have to declare to a professional body later;
  • for students on a visa, serious trouble that can follow from losing your place.

A record of misconduct can also surface in references and fitness-to-practise checks long after you have left.

How cheating gets noticed

SEB is only one layer, and it does not claim to catch everything. Universities use several things together, and a plan that works against one often fails against another:

  • Server-side checks. Moodle can require that the exam is opened in a genuine, correctly configured SEB, and it keeps logs of each attempt: when you logged in, how you moved through the quiz, how long each question took, and from which network address.
  • Invigilators. In a room, or by webcam, people are watching, and they know what a normal exam looks like.
  • Looking at the answers afterwards. Similar wrong answers, answers that match another student’s order of mistakes, text that does not sound like your usual writing, or a sudden jump in marks can all be flagged for review. Question banks that give each student different questions in a different order make copying easier to spot.
  • Other students. Cheating that involves a second person, a group chat or a paid service leaves messages and payments behind, and people talk.
  • Later reports. Cases are sometimes reopened months afterwards when new evidence appears.

Not every case is caught, and nobody should claim otherwise. But “most people won’t notice” is a poor bet when the stakes are your degree.

The hidden risk: “bypass” tools and services

Searches for ways around exam software lead to videos, forum posts, downloadable programs and paid “services”. Many of these are dangerous in ways that have nothing to do with the exam:

  • Malware. Programs that promise to disable exam software are a well-known way to spread password-stealing and remote-access malware. Installing one can give a stranger control of the computer that holds your coursework, photos and bank logins.
  • Stolen accounts. Anything that asks for your university login can use it to read your email and files, or to sell the account.
  • Scams and blackmail. Paid “exam help” and contract-cheating services often take the money and disappear. Some then threaten to report you to your university unless you pay again.
  • A trail. Your payment, your messages and your downloaded files are evidence that you chose to cheat.

Using AI to answer exam questions

If an exam does not allow generative AI, using it is cheating, whichever way you reach it. It is also a poor tactic: AI answers can be confidently wrong, they often look alike from one student to the next, and they will not teach you what the next module assumes you know.

Also worth knowing

  • A fault is not cheating. If SEB or your internet fails, say so straight away. The invigilator and exam office deal with this often, and being honest is always better. See what to do if SEB won’t start or quit.
  • Trying to cheat can be treated as misconduct even if it fails. Many regulations cover an attempt, not only a result.
  • Helping someone else cheat counts too. Sharing the quit password, passing answers on or sitting an exam for a friend are all covered.

If you feel you can’t cope

Most cheating comes from pressure: a missed deadline, fear of failing, family or health trouble. There are almost always better options, and they are quicker to arrange than most people think:

  • Tell your university early. Ask about extensions, mitigating circumstances, deferring the exam or a resit. These routes exist because everyone has a bad term sometimes.
  • Ask for help with the subject. Lecturers, tutors, peer support and academic skills services.
  • Ask for a wellbeing or disability adjustment if you need extra time, a different room or other arrangements.
  • If you have already done something you regret, speak to your personal tutor or a student advice service (many are run by the students’ union) before an investigation starts. Admitting a problem early is usually treated more favourably than being found out.

Getting ready the honest way

Run through the student checklist and do a practice quiz in SEB. Confident preparation is the best protection against both technical problems and the temptation to take a risk.
